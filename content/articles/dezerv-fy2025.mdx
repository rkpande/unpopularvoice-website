## The Number That Should Haunt the Next Investor Pitch

There is one specific number in the four-year history that matters more than any other to how you read Dezerv.

In FY2023, Dezerv Investments (the parent entity, standalone) posted ₹52.58 Cr in revenue against ₹58.40 Cr in costs. The loss was ₹4.71 Cr. Call it ₹5 Cr on ₹53 Cr. The company was 9 paise from break-even. For a two-year-old wealth-management firm in India, that is extraordinary. It is the kind of early-year result that validates the founder thesis: you can build a modern wealth platform in India without the decade-long runway that traditional wealth managers assume.

Two years later, the parent is at ₹34.60 Cr of standalone revenue (down from ₹52.58 Cr) and ₹111.90 Cr of standalone loss (up 24x from ₹4.71 Cr).

Two possible readings of the same four-year arc.

Reading one: FY2023 was an aberration driven by a favourable fee structure that SEBI subsequently revised; the business always needed a bigger cost base to scale, and FY2024 and FY2025 are the normalisation years of a deliberate investment cycle.

Reading two: FY2023 proved the model worked at a smaller scale; FY2024 and FY2025 show the cost base was expanded faster than revenue could follow, and the FY2023 proof-point is now four years and ₹160 Cr of cumulative loss in the rear-view.

Both readings are compatible with the audit. Which one is correct will be settled by the FY2026 and FY2027 filings, not by press releases.

## What AUM Does ₹44 Cr in Fees Imply?

This is the question the filing does not answer directly, and is the one most investors will want to triangulate. Wealth-management revenue is overwhelmingly a function of assets under management (AUM). If you know the fee rate, you can back-solve AUM from revenue. If you know AUM, you can judge whether the cost base is proportionate to the opportunity.

Dezerv's FY2025 revenue breakdown, consolidated: fees and commission income ₹44 Cr, interest income ₹16.8 Cr, fair value gains ₹4.8 Cr. The fees line is the one that scales with AUM.

Three scenarios for the fee rate.

**At a 1.5% blended fee** (typical for PMS-heavy wealth platforms with discretionary mandates), ₹44 Cr of fees implies AUM of approximately ₹2,900 Cr.

**At a 1.0% blended fee** (more typical for advisory-plus-distribution models), ₹44 Cr implies AUM of approximately ₹4,400 Cr.

**At a 0.5% blended fee** (closer to distribution-heavy or robo-advisory economics), ₹44 Cr implies AUM of approximately ₹8,800 Cr.

Public reporting suggests Dezerv's actual AUM is in the ₹8,000 to ₹10,000 Cr range, which would put the implied blended fee closer to 0.5% than to the PMS-heavy rate. This implies the business model is skewed more toward distribution, trail commissions, and lower-fee advisory than toward high-margin discretionary PMS. That mix choice matters because it sets an upper ceiling on revenue per unit of AUM, and therefore on the AUM growth required to make the current cost base profitable.

The filing does not disclose AUM directly. That number sits in the SEBI PMS disclosure system and is not a required public disclosure in the Companies Act filing we work from here. What the filing does establish is the revenue achieved, and what the audit does not establish is the AUM behind it. The two together are the missing pair.

## Where Dezerv Sits in the Wealth-Tech Category

Indian wealth-tech in FY25 is not a single category. It is a cluster of structurally different businesses that all describe themselves as "digital wealth" to the same customer.

**Pure distribution models** (Kuvera, ET Money, Groww Mutual Funds): trail commission on regular mutual-fund plans, low fees, scale-driven. Revenue per AUM rupee is small; AUM required for profitability is large (tens of thousands of crore). These businesses rarely cross profitability under ₹20,000 Cr of AUM.

**Discretionary PMS and advisory** (traditional wealth managers transitioning digital; Dezerv-adjacent): management fees on discretionary portfolios, higher fee rate, lower volume. Profitability achievable at smaller AUM scale but requires concentrated high-ticket clients (₹1 crore plus investable assets).

**Robo-advisory and model-portfolio platforms** (Smallcase, Scripbox): subscription or basis-point fees on execution; closer to SaaS economics than wealth economics; margin depends on retention more than AUM scale.

**Full-stack new-age private banks** (INDmoney and similar): aggregation of banking, broking, mutual funds, and insurance under one app; revenue mix includes broking float and distribution commissions in addition to advisory fees.

Dezerv sits closest to the second bucket in positioning but, based on the implied fee rate reverse-engineered above, is executing closer to the first. The founder team (ex-IIFL Wealth) has traditional PMS/advisory roots, so the brand positions as discretionary wealth; the economic reality in the filing suggests a more distribution-heavy mix.

That tension shows up in the cost line. Discretionary wealth justifies high relationship-manager salaries and small-team economics. Distribution-heavy models typically cannot carry that cost base profitably because the fee rate is a fraction of discretionary. A ₹111 Cr payroll is a discretionary-wealth cost structure; a ~0.5% blended fee rate is a distribution economic reality. The gap between the two is one way to read the FY25 loss.

## Three Entities: What Each Does

**Dezerv Investments Private Limited**, the parent. SEBI Investment Adviser and PMS registrations. Investment team, technology, and client relationships sit here. **Fact:** standalone FY2025 revenue ₹34.60 Cr; standalone loss ₹111.90 Cr.

**Dezerv Distribution Services Private Limited**, SEBI mutual fund distributor. Earns trail commissions on AUM in regular mutual fund plans. Revenue compounds with AUM.

**Dezerv Securities Private Limited**, SEBI stockbroker. Executes trades for PMS clients. Revenue tied to portfolio turnover.

**On subsidiary economics, Estimated:** Consolidated profit after tax (PAT) (−₹111.98 Cr) minus standalone PAT (−₹111.90 Cr) implies combined subsidiary incremental loss of ~₹0.08 Cr against ~₹31 Cr in incremental revenue. This suggests near-breakeven operations. Caveat: intercompany charges and consolidation eliminations may affect this derived figure.

## FY2024: The Revenue Collapse

**Fact:** Standalone revenue fell from ₹52.58 Cr to ₹20.06 Cr. The filing states no reason.

**Likely explanation 1, regulatory:** SEBI's 2023 mutual fund distributor expense ratio changes reduced trail commissions industry-wide. Distribution-dependent revenue would have been directly affected.

**Likely explanation 2, structural:** Shifting clients from distribution to PMS creates a gap year. Distribution generates recurring commissions on existing AUM. PMS management fees accumulate on a new base and take time to ramp.

**Likely explanation 3, subsidiary ring-fencing:** If FY2024 was when distribution revenue moved into Dezerv Distribution, the parent's standalone line would show the decline while consolidated remained more stable. The FY2024 standalone (₹20.06 Cr) vs. consolidated (~₹26 Cr) gap supports this.

**Unknown from filings:** The actual reason is not in the available documents.

## The ₹111 Cr Salary Line

**Fact:** Consolidated employee costs were ₹111 Cr, 76% up from ₹63 Cr in FY2024. Standalone parent: ₹92.60 Cr. **Estimated:** subsidiaries ~₹18.4 Cr.

**Likely explanation:** Wealth management is people-before-revenue. Portfolio managers and relationship managers must be hired before the AUM they will service arrives.

**Counterargument:** A 76% increase does not require 76% headcount growth. A portion may reflect employee stock option plan (ESOP) charges (accounting expense, not cash) or compensation resets for existing employees.

**Unknown from filings:** The split between new hires, pay changes, and ESOP charges is not available.

## The Math of Recovery

What has to happen for Dezerv's consolidated P&L to reach break-even?

The current gap is ₹112 Cr of annual loss against ₹65.6 Cr of revenue. Closing the gap requires some combination of revenue growth, cost-growth deceleration, and operating-leverage improvement.

Set up the arithmetic.

Assume the existing cost base of ₹178 Cr is largely fixed in the near term. Payroll (₹111 Cr) and software (₹8 Cr) are both commitments that cannot be unwound quickly. Marketing (₹30 Cr) is the one line that can flex. If total costs grow at say 25% a year (consistent with slowing but continued investment), FY2026 costs land near ₹222 Cr, FY2027 near ₹278 Cr.

For revenue to catch up: FY2026 revenue would need to reach ₹222 Cr for break-even, which is 3.4x FY2025. FY2027 would need ₹278 Cr. On the current fee economics (reverse-engineered above at approximately 0.5% blended), that would require AUM moving from ₹8,000-10,000 Cr today to roughly ₹44,000-56,000 Cr by FY2027. That is a 4x to 6x AUM growth in two years at the scale where AUM growth typically slows.

The alternative path is improving the fee rate by shifting mix toward discretionary PMS. Lifting the blended fee from 0.5% to 1.0% halves the required AUM growth. That mix shift, if executed, is the strategic bet that makes the FY2025 cost ramp defensible.

What the filing does not tell us: the current AUM number, the discretionary-versus-distribution split, the per-client revenue trajectory, or the retention rate. All four would be needed to model the recovery with confidence. The filing establishes the end state of the P&L; the path from here requires disclosure that sits in regulatory submissions outside the annual company audit.

## Three Futures for Dezerv

A simple scenario frame for FY2026 and beyond, based entirely on what the filing allows us to reason about.

**The bull case.** Revenue doubles again (FY2026 consolidated ~₹130 Cr). Cost growth decelerates to 25% (FY2026 costs ~₹222 Cr). Loss widens modestly to ~₹90 Cr but revenue coverage of cost improves from 37% in FY25 to 59% in FY26. A fresh equity round of $50 to $75 million in FY2026 or early FY2027 at a materially higher valuation than the last round. Break-even in sight by FY2028.

**The base case.** Revenue grows 70% to ~₹112 Cr. Costs grow 40% to ~₹249 Cr. Loss widens to ~₹130 Cr. Revenue coverage improves to 45%. A fresh equity round in FY2026 at a flat-to-slightly-up valuation, led by existing investors. Break-even remains 3-4 years out.

**The bear case.** Revenue grows 40% to ~₹92 Cr as AUM growth slows. Costs grow 50% to ~₹267 Cr as earlier hires fully load. Loss widens to ~₹170 Cr. Revenue coverage falls to 34%. A down-round becomes necessary or a strategic exit (sale to a larger wealth-tech or bank-affiliated platform) enters conversation.

Which case lands depends on three variables the audit does not disclose and the founder team controls: AUM growth trajectory, mix shift toward discretionary PMS, and employee-cost discipline (whether the ₹111 Cr ramp turns into a plateau or continues at 50% plus annual growth).

The filing records the end of FY25. It does not yet record which future.
