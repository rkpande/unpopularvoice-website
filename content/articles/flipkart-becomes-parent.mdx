The consumer-marketplace entity you order from used to be a subsidiary.

It is now the parent.

In December 2025, India's National Company Law Tribunal sanctioned a composite Scheme of Arrangement that merged three Singapore-incorporated Flipkart holding companies into Flipkart Internet Private Limited, the Bengaluru-registered entity that runs the Flipkart consumer app. The reverse flip from Singapore to India completed in March 2026.

The entity most people think of as "the Flipkart app" is now, legally, the top of the entire Flipkart group in India.

This matters because it changes who is answerable, under which legal regime, for the group's reporting, taxation, and governance. For almost a decade, the Flipkart group's apex entity was Flipkart Private Limited, a Singapore company. Indian audited financials were filed one layer down. Group-level consolidation lived offshore. With the flip complete, the apex entity is Indian. Group-level consolidation will land in India. The next annual audit will be the first group-consolidated filing under Indian accounting standards (Ind AS) ever produced by the Flipkart group.

The three-layer chain through Singapore existed for a specific historical reason. When Flipkart's early investors funded the business between 2009 and 2014, they wanted a jurisdiction with familiar contract law, flexible share-class rules, and tax-efficient exit pathways. Singapore was the default choice for Indian internet companies of that era. Ola, Freshworks, Razorpay, Paytm, PhonePe, Zomato, and most other India-first consumer-internet businesses of the period incorporated their holding companies offshore for similar reasons.

That calculus has reversed. SEBI's listing framework now requires a company that intends to IPO on an Indian exchange to have its primary holding company inside India. The 2024 Union Budget further eased the tax friction that used to penalise reverse-flip transactions, making the move economically viable for the first time at scale. PhonePe did the flip in 2022. Razorpay began its process in 2024. Groww completed it in 2025. Flipkart's flip is the largest and most structurally complex of the Indian cohort, because the group architecture includes eight distinct entities across logistics, fashion, payments, health, and sourcing, each of which had to be collapsed through a single composite scheme.

## The FY25 audit shows how the flip was prepared

The reverse flip is a 2025-26 event, not a FY25 event. But the FY25 audit for Flipkart Internet, signed on 11 June 2025 by Walker Chandiok & Co LLP, records the preparatory architecture that made it possible. Three movements are visible inside the audit note on related-party transactions and share capital.

The first is a rights issue of ₹3,249 Cr completed during FY25, with the counterparties being Flipkart Marketplace Private Limited and Flipkart Private Limited (both Singapore). Walmart equity flowed down the holding chain into the India operating entity. A rights issue of this size from the parent into an operating subsidiary is not, by itself, unusual for a loss-making e-commerce entity. What is unusual is the counterparty set: the same two Singapore entities that would be absorbed into Flipkart Internet six months later. The rights issue fattened Flipkart Internet's balance sheet ahead of the scheme by converting what would otherwise have been intercompany loans into pure equity. When the Singapore holding companies subsequently merged into Flipkart Internet, there was no intercompany debt to unwind on the apex entity; the equity had already been injected.

The second is a flow of demand loans. During FY25, Flipkart Internet took ₹24,753 Cr of demand loans from its fellow subsidiary Flipkart India Private Limited (the B2B wholesale layer), of which ₹24,133 Cr was repaid within the year. The gross intercompany flow is large; the net outstanding at year-end is modest. The structural point is that Flipkart Internet was being funded in large volume by Flipkart India throughout the year. For context, ₹24,753 Cr is approximately 121% of Flipkart Internet's own annual revenue of ₹20,493 Cr. A loan flow of that magnitude between two sister entities, repaid within the year, is operationally equivalent to using Flipkart India as a working-capital treasury for Flipkart Internet. Nothing about this is illegal or hidden; the amounts are explicitly disclosed in the related-party-transactions note. But it does reveal that the two entities function, in practical cash-management terms, as one business with one cash pool.

The third is in the post-year-end notes: an allotment of 24.7 million additional Class A equity shares via a further rights issue, and a bonus issue of 2.91 billion Class A shares and 12.4 million Class B shares in a 101:1 ratio, by capitalisation of the securities premium account. That bonus split reset the per-share arithmetic. Before the bonus, Flipkart Internet had approximately 29 million equity shares outstanding at Re. 1 face value, a share count small enough that per-share metrics would be nonsensical for public reporting. After the 101:1 bonus, the share count expanded roughly 100x, producing a count appropriate for public listing. The entity is now technically ready to IPO from a share-structure standpoint. This is a mechanical pre-IPO housekeeping move; it does not change the economic value held by any shareholder, but it signals the timeline.

Together, these three movements set up what the NCLT would sanction in December.

## How the scheme of arrangement actually works, mechanically

The composite scheme that NCLT sanctioned on 12 December 2025 is not a simple merger. It is a six-step sequence that merges eight distinct legal entities into one, across two jurisdictions, under two different court systems.

**Step 1.** Flipkart Health Private Limited, Quickroutes International Private Limited, KLICK2SHOP Logistics Services International Private Limited, and Flippay Private Limited merge into Flipkart Marketplace Private Limited (Singapore). Four smaller operating entities fold into one of the two Singapore holding companies.

**Step 2.** FK Myntra Holdings Private Limited and Flipkart Investments Private Limited merge into Flipkart Private Limited (Singapore). The two holding-tier Singapore entities absorb the two intermediate holding entities.

**Step 3.** Flipkart Marketplace Private Limited (Singapore), now carrying the four absorbed operating entities, merges into Flipkart Private Limited (Singapore). All operating and intermediate holding assets are now consolidated into the single Singapore apex.

**Step 4.** Flipkart Private Limited (Singapore) merges into Flipkart Internet Private Limited (India). The Singapore apex transfers all its assets, liabilities, and share capital to the Indian subsidiary. At this moment, Flipkart Internet legally inherits the group's entire asset base.

**Step 5.** Fresh shares of Flipkart Internet are issued to Walmart Inc. (and historical minority shareholders who were on the Flipkart Private Limited register) in exchange for the cancellation of their Singapore shares. The ultimate beneficial ownership of the group is unchanged. The legal domicile is now India.

**Step 6.** Flipkart Private Limited (Singapore) and all other merged Singapore entities are wound up. The Singapore holding chain ceases to exist.

Each of these steps required separate regulatory approvals: from the NCLT (Bengaluru bench) for the Indian leg, from the General Division of the High Court of the Republic of Singapore for the Singapore leg, and from the Reserve Bank of India under the Foreign Exchange Management Act for the capital-control implications of transferring assets across borders. Flipkart also applied for clearance under Press Note 3 (Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade), which requires government approval for investments originating from countries sharing a land border with India. This is triggered by Tencent's historical shareholding in Flipkart (held through a Singapore vehicle), which the flip has to be structured to accommodate.

The scheme took approximately seven months from NCLT lodgement (22 May 2025) to NCLT sanction (12 December 2025). That timeline is considered fast by Indian corporate-restructuring standards for a scheme of this complexity; PhonePe's comparable flip took approximately eleven months, and Razorpay's is still in progress.

Revenue grew 14%. Net loss narrowed 37%. Employee cost was actually cut 8% while the top line grew. Three movements in the same direction, in the same year, in favour of operating leverage.

Revenue mix tells the next part. Marketplace services (seller commissions) more than doubled to ₹7,751 Cr, up from ₹3,734 Cr. Advertising services grew 27% to ₹6,317 Cr. Logistics income fell 38% to ₹4,225 Cr, as the Ekart logistics arm absorbed more of that revenue line into its own separately-filed entity. The marketplace is extracting more platform-fee revenue and shedding lower-margin shipping revenue.

The composition change is material for IPO positioning. Platform-fee revenue (marketplace commission plus advertising, together ₹14,068 Cr) carries structurally higher gross margins than logistics-service revenue. Marketplace commission has near-zero direct cost of revenue once platform infrastructure is built. Advertising revenue is even cleaner: it carries only ad-ops, attribution, and inventory-management cost, all of which scale sub-linearly with revenue. Logistics revenue, by contrast, carries a near one-to-one cost of revenue (warehousing, last-mile, returns). Shedding logistics revenue from the P&L while retaining the economic benefit (through Ekart, which still serves Flipkart sellers) is a straightforward margin-engineering move.

That is a defensible operating story for an IPO prospectus. It is also the number that will carry forward into the first post-flip audit, when Flipkart Internet consolidates the entities that used to sit above it.

## What the current loss figure does and doesn't tell you

The headline ₹1,494 Cr loss for FY25 is Flipkart Internet's consolidated figure (the entity plus its own subsidiaries); its standalone loss was ₹1,569 Cr. Either way, it covers Flipkart Internet, not its sister entities. What it does not include:

- The ₹5,189 Cr FY25 loss booked at Flipkart India (the wholesale arm). That sits on a separate P&L.
- The losses at Ekart (the logistics entity, a separately-filed subsidiary). Press coverage citing the Ekart FY25 filing puts that loss at approximately ₹1,508 Cr.
- The P&L of Myntra, which sits as a sister entity. Myntra posted a FY25 profit of ₹548 Cr, a notable 18x improvement year-on-year.
- The P&L of Flipkart Health, Shopsy, Flipkart Health Plus, and the various payments / advertising subsidiaries that fall under the group umbrella.

The group-level consolidation, which the next audit will produce for the first time at the India entity level, will combine these numbers with appropriate intercompany eliminations. Analyst estimates (not UV's) suggest the combined group loss across all Flipkart India operating entities for FY25 is in the range of ₹6,000 Cr to ₹8,000 Cr after eliminations, driven primarily by the wholesale and logistics layers. That number is the one investors in the eventual IPO will be looking at. It is not the ₹1,494 Cr loss for Flipkart Internet, which is the entity-level figure only.

Revenue grew 17% to ₹82,787 Cr. Net loss widened 22% to ₹5,189 Cr. Stock-in-trade purchases rose to ₹87,738 Cr. Finance costs rose 57% to ₹454 Cr (predominantly intercompany interest).

Flipkart India is the inventory engine of the group. It buys physical goods at wholesale and supplies them into the marketplace sellers' listings. Loss widening with revenue is the structural norm for an inventory-led wholesale operation, where there is little operating leverage to extract. The FY25 numbers reflect that reality rather than any specific deterioration.

Post-flip, this entity continues to operate under the same legal form. Its position in the group has moved: it now sits as a fellow Indian subsidiary alongside, rather than operationally parallel to, the renamed consumer-marketplace entity above it. The inventory-cycle funding relationship between Flipkart Internet and Flipkart India will continue, but will now be fully internal to the India tax and reporting regime rather than routed through Singapore for upstream reconciliation.

## What the Walmart funding model looks like on the register

For a group of Flipkart's scale, posting the combined losses that it posted in FY25, the next line to check would normally be the external debt register.

Across both operating entities, external secured facilities are largely absent through the FY23-to-FY25 loss cycle. The ₹454 Cr of finance cost at Flipkart India is predominantly intercompany interest paid upstream to the Walmart-controlled Singapore entities. The ₹162 Cr of finance cost at Flipkart Internet includes a significant intercompany-interest component (approximately ₹120 Cr) with the remainder being lease and working-capital charges. The combined ₹5,474 Cr of equity reportedly infused by Walmart's Singapore entities into Flipkart Internet during FY25 (including the ₹3,249 Cr rights issue recorded in the audit and additional post-balance-sheet infusions) is the mechanism that absorbs the loss.

That is the parent-funded shape. The trade-off is not on the P&L; the operating entities carry no external debt-servicing obligation. The trade-off is strategic: the business depends on sustained Walmart willingness to deploy equity into the India structure, year after year. The FY25 numbers show that willingness remained intact through the preparatory period for the flip.

Post-flip, that funding model will have to change. An IPO-bound company cannot indefinitely rely on parent equity to cover standalone operating losses; public-market investors will underwrite only a bounded runway of continued dilution. What changes mechanically at listing is that Walmart's ability to absorb losses through recurring rights issues becomes constrained by SEBI's framework around related-party transactions and by the shareholder-approval requirements that attach to large preferential allotments. Flipkart will have to demonstrate, inside the first two post-listing years, either operating cash-flow positivity at the group level or a clear path to it on a forecastable trajectory. The loss-narrowing shown in the FY25 marketplace entity is one data point in that direction. The widening loss at the wholesale entity is a counter-data-point. The group-level consolidation will resolve which direction the net picture is moving in.

## Why this matters for the IPO investor

An Indian IPO investor who looks at Flipkart will encounter a consolidation structure that is new at the India layer. Previously, anyone wanting a group picture had to reference Walmart's 10-K, which disclosed Flipkart as a segment with limited granularity. From the first post-flip audit onwards, the group consolidation will sit inside an Indian annual report, with the full Ind AS disclosure regime: segment reporting, related-party transactions, auditor key-audit-matters, and the full balance-sheet detail that Walmart's parent-level reporting does not surface.

That new transparency cuts both ways. It allows Indian analysts to model Flipkart with proper audit-level data for the first time. It also exposes the group to the type of forensic reading that Indian loss-making consumer-tech IPOs (Paytm, Zomato, Nykaa, PB Fintech) have faced from day one of listing: deep analysis of related-party flows, scrutiny of segment profitability, and tight attention to the rate at which equity burn matches revenue growth.

The specific arithmetic that will attract that attention: a group with ~₹1,00,000 Cr of combined revenue after eliminations, ~₹6,000-8,000 Cr of consolidated loss, ~₹20,000-25,000 Cr of cumulative equity infused by Walmart since 2018, and a funding model that has, until now, relied on the Singapore holding chain to smooth capital flow. All of that now has to be presented in a single Indian annual report.

## The read the FY25 audit gives you

The FY25 audit for Flipkart Internet does three jobs at once.

It records an operating story: revenue up 14%, loss narrowed 37%, employee cost cut 8%, revenue mix shifted toward platform fees. That story reads well as the baseline for an IPO prospectus.

It records a structural preparation: a ₹3,249 Cr rights issue, ₹24,753 Cr of intercompany lending, a 101:1 bonus share split, and the lodgement of a composite Scheme of Arrangement with two courts. That preparation set up the NCLT order that followed in December 2025.

And it records the entity in its last full year as a subsidiary. By the time the next audit lands, Flipkart Internet will be a consolidating holding company for the Flipkart group in India, and the Singapore entities that previously sat above it will be gone.

The consumer app absorbed its own parents. The FY25 numbers are the last snapshot of the Flipkart you knew. The next audit will be the first snapshot of the Flipkart that is listed.

Everything above is on the public record. The audits landed. The numbers are the numbers. The structure is the story most readers do not see.
