The Flipkart app grew revenue 14% in FY25.

Its net loss fell 37%.

That is the summary line in the FY25 audit for Flipkart Internet Private Limited, the legal entity behind the Flipkart consumer marketplace.

But a summary line is a poor way to read a loss-narrowing year. Revenue can grow by burning cash on acquisition. Losses can narrow because of a one-time tax credit or a write-back. The question that matters is which specific levers actually compressed the loss, whether those levers are repeatable, and whether the operating story is strong enough to sustain itself when the Singapore holding-chain funding that cushioned the entity through earlier loss years is no longer the primary bridge.

Three lines in the audit tell you what happened. Read in order, they connect the dots.

## Line 1: Revenue from operations, ₹20,493 Cr

Up 14.4% from ₹17,907 Cr in FY24. The consolidated figure, matching what the audit's P&L records.

But the composition is more interesting than the headline. The mix inside the audit's revenue note tells a different story than the top-line number.

Marketplace services, the commission Flipkart charges sellers on transactions, more than doubled. Advertising revenue, which Flipkart earns from seller-funded promotion inside the app, grew 27%. Those are the two highest-margin lines in the mix.

Logistics income fell 38%. The structural reason is that Ekart, the Flipkart logistics arm, now carries more of that revenue line in its own separately filed entity. The marketplace still earns the economics but no longer books all of the shipping fees as its own revenue.

The mechanics behind the marketplace-commission doubling deserve a closer read. For most of Flipkart's history, commission rates on seller transactions were deliberately held low to drive seller onboarding and GMV growth. Two things changed in FY25. First, Flipkart raised commission rates across multiple product categories, bringing them closer to Amazon's benchmarks (which have historically been 8-15% depending on category). Second, the mix of transactions shifted toward categories where commission rates are structurally higher: fashion, beauty, home, and lifestyle, where Myntra-adjacent inventory expanded aggressively. The ₹4,017 Cr of incremental marketplace-services revenue (from ₹3,734 Cr to ₹7,751 Cr) comes from the combination of rate action plus category-mix shift, not pure transaction-volume growth.

Advertising revenue is a different story. The 27% growth to ₹6,317 Cr reflects the maturation of Flipkart's sponsored-listings advertising product. Sellers now bid for prominence on search and category pages, and Flipkart takes a cost-per-click or cost-per-impression fee. This revenue line is almost pure margin; the marginal cost of serving one more ad impression is effectively zero once the ad-tech infrastructure is built. Advertising revenue is now 31% of total revenue from operations (₹6,317 Cr of ₹20,493 Cr), up from 28% in FY24 and from approximately 20% in FY22. That trajectory, if it continues, is the single most important lever in Flipkart's path to profitability.

## Line 2: Net loss, ₹1,494 Cr

Down 37% from ₹2,359 Cr in FY24. The sharpest single-year loss compression in the entity's recent history.

The standalone number is slightly larger (loss narrowed 32% from ₹2,296 Cr to ₹1,569 Cr). The ₹75 Cr gap between standalone and consolidated reflects the share of profit from subsidiaries and associates, which has grown modestly.

What matters is the direction and the magnitude. The loss did not narrow because of a one-time gain or a tax credit. It narrowed because the operating expense base moved in a specific way.

For a cleaner read, compare the three-year history. In FY23, Flipkart Internet posted a loss of ₹4,845 Cr. In FY24, that narrowed to ₹2,359 Cr, a 51% reduction. In FY25, it narrowed further to ₹1,494 Cr, a 37% reduction. The compression rate has slowed from FY23-to-FY24 to FY24-to-FY25, but it has continued in the same direction for two consecutive years. A straight-line extrapolation would put FY26 at approximately ₹950-1,000 Cr and FY27 at approximately ₹600-700 Cr. Those are not forecasts (straight-line extrapolations rarely hold) but they give a frame for what break-even would look like if the current trajectory continues. The implied break-even year, if trajectory holds, is FY28 or FY29.

The important caveat is that the FY25 compression was partly engineered by shedding logistics revenue (which carries a near one-to-one cost) into a separately filed subsidiary. Some of the loss-narrowing is not operational improvement at the marketplace; it is structural reclassification. The next audit, which will consolidate the group under the post-flip architecture, will reunify the logistics P&L with the marketplace P&L and show the combined picture.

## Line 3: Employee benefit expense, ₹4,748 Cr

Down 8.3% from ₹5,178 Cr in FY24. On a revenue base that grew 14.4%.

That is the single most important movement in the P&L. For a marketplace entity, employee cost is one of the largest controllable expense lines. Cutting it by ₹430 Cr while scaling revenue by ₹2,586 Cr produces operating leverage that compounds through the P&L.

Depreciation also declined, from ₹317 Cr to ₹256 Cr. Finance costs declined marginally from ₹175 Cr to ₹162 Cr. Other expenses rose from ₹14,958 Cr to ₹17,145 Cr, broadly in line with revenue growth. Advertising and promotional spend specifically within other expenses jumped 37% from ₹2,996 Cr to ₹4,100 Cr.

The employee-cost cut requires a mechanism. It did not come from mass layoffs (Flipkart's publicly reported headcount reductions in FY25 were modest, approximately 1,100 roles out of a ~25,000 employee base, which would account for only ₹150-200 Cr at Flipkart's average compensation levels). The bulk of the ₹430 Cr reduction came from two other factors. The first is ESOP charges: Flipkart's employee stock option expense, which runs through employee benefit expense as a non-cash item, declined meaningfully year-on-year as earlier-vintage grants vested out and new grants were structured with longer vesting cliffs. The second is the structural shedding of some employee cost into Ekart and other subsidiaries, which happens in parallel to the logistics-revenue shedding described above.

The combination of these effects means the ₹430 Cr employee-cost cut is partly operational and partly mechanical. A reader who models forward based on "employee cost will continue to decline" should factor in that the mechanical component is non-repeating.

## Read together, the audit tells you how the loss compressed

Revenue grew 14%. Employee cost fell 8%. Depreciation fell 19%. Finance cost fell. Advertising rose 37%, more than offsetting the mix shift benefit from marketplace commission. But the overall expense base grew only 8%, well below the 14% revenue growth. That gap is the loss narrowing.

The audit records the operating lever: for the first time in recent years, the Flipkart app grew revenue faster than its cost base. That is the arithmetic of a marketplace approaching reported accounting break-even.

## The Amazon comparison, in the same category

A useful external calibration: Amazon Seller Services Private Limited, the Amazon India marketplace operating entity, filed its FY25 audit around the same time. Amazon's standalone revenue was ₹30,139 Cr, up 19% year-on-year. Amazon's standalone loss was ₹374 Cr, down 89% from ₹3,470 Cr in FY24. Amazon's operating cash flow was +₹5,063 Cr, up 7x. Amazon approached reported accounting break-even at the marketplace layer in FY25.

Flipkart Internet's FY25 figures (₹20,493 Cr revenue, ₹1,494 Cr loss) look weaker than Amazon's on a headline basis but need a structural adjustment. Amazon Seller Services keeps logistics revenue (delivery charges) within the entity; Flipkart Internet has shed much of that to Ekart. On a like-for-like basis, consolidating Flipkart Internet with the pro-rata share of Ekart that serves Flipkart sellers would produce a revenue figure closer to ₹28,000-30,000 Cr and a loss figure closer to ₹2,500-3,000 Cr. The gap between Flipkart and Amazon at the marketplace layer is real, but it is narrower than the single-entity numbers suggest, and it is closing.

The strategic context is also different. Amazon India has deployed ₹55,337 Cr of cumulative equity since inception, against ₹45,650 Cr of cumulative retained losses. Flipkart (across the two main operating entities and all subsidiaries) has drawn cumulative equity of a similar order of magnitude. Both businesses operate on the same funding thesis: that the eventual steady-state margin of a dominant Indian e-commerce marketplace justifies the cumulative loss required to build it.

## What the audit also shows: how the loss is funded

For a marketplace entity of Flipkart's scale, the next line to check is the balance sheet. Where did the FY25 loss cushion come from, and what is the debt profile?

The audit note on share capital and reserves records a rights issue during FY25 of ₹3,249 Cr. The counterparties: Flipkart Marketplace Private Limited and Flipkart Private Limited, both Singapore-incorporated, both ultimately held by Walmart Inc. This is Walmart equity flowing into the India operating entity.

The audit also records demand loans taken during FY25 from Flipkart India Private Limited (the fellow subsidiary and B2B wholesale layer) of ₹24,753 Cr, of which ₹24,133 Cr was repaid within the year. The intercompany flow is large, but the net outstanding at year-end is modest. The structural point is that the wholesale entity funds working capital for the marketplace entity on a rolling basis.

External secured debt against Flipkart Internet is largely absent through this cycle. The ₹162 Cr of finance cost in FY25 includes intercompany interest paid to Flipkart India (₹120 Cr noted explicitly in the cash flow decomposition), lease liability interest, and some working-capital charges. No new scheduled bank facility was taken against the entity during the FY23-FY25 loss cycle.

Paid-up equity capital at Flipkart Internet stood at approximately ₹28,500 Cr as of 31 March 2025, with securities premium of substantially more. The capital base is large enough, in absolute terms, to absorb multiple years of additional loss at the current trajectory without the entity breaching net-worth erosion thresholds. The parent-funded model, while not indefinitely sustainable, has operational runway that is measured in years, not quarters.

## Why the trajectory matters for the IPO

The post-flip Flipkart is widely expected to file its DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) with SEBI during calendar 2026 or early 2027, targeting a listing at a valuation in the $35-50 billion range. The prospectus will present three fiscal years of audited financials as the primary disclosure. FY25 will be the earliest of those three years. FY26 and FY27 will follow.

For SEBI's approval path and for investor demand, the key question is whether the FY25-to-FY26 and FY26-to-FY27 trajectories continue the loss-narrowing direction visible in the FY25 audit. If they do, Flipkart presents as a pre-break-even growth story with a clear path. If they stall or reverse, the IPO narrative gets materially harder.

The specific metrics investors will scrutinise: (a) marketplace-commission revenue growth rate, that is, whether the 108% FY24-to-FY25 jump was a one-time rate-action effect or a sustainable new baseline; (b) advertising revenue as a percentage of total, and whether the current ~31% continues to expand; (c) the standalone-vs-consolidated gap after the post-flip group consolidation lands; and (d) whether net working-capital absorbs cash or releases it (marketplace models typically release working capital as they scale, but logistics-adjacent models absorb it). The FY25 audit gives directional comfort on all four, without giving conclusive comfort on any of them.

## The two things the audit does not tell you

The first is what is happening at the sister entity. Flipkart India Private Limited, the B2B wholesale engine, filed its own FY25 audit with S.R. Batliboi & Associates LLP. That audit has its own P&L, with revenue of ₹82,787 Cr (up 17%) and a loss of ₹5,189 Cr (widened 22%). Reading Flipkart only through Flipkart Internet misses the entire inventory layer that supplies the marketplace.

The second is what happens next. The audit's post-year-end notes record three material items after 31 March 2025: a composite Scheme of Arrangement lodged with the NCLT and the Singapore High Court on 22 May 2025 (covering eight group entities), a further rights issue of 24.7 million Class A shares, and a 101:1 bonus share split. The NCLT sanctioned the scheme in December 2025. The reverse flip from Singapore to India completed in March 2026. Flipkart Internet is now the principal holding company for the entire Flipkart group in India, ahead of a planned Indian IPO.

The FY25 audit is the last filed snapshot of the entity in its pre-flip form. The next audit will look different: Flipkart Internet will consolidate the entities that used to sit above it in the Singapore holding chain, and the per-share arithmetic will have reset.

What the FY25 audit records clearly, however, is the operational direction. Revenue grew. Costs were disciplined. Loss narrowed sharply. The marketplace is closer to break-even than it has ever been. That part of the story will carry forward into the next audit regardless of how the group architecture has moved around it.
