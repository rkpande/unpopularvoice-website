A year ago, this site read Flipkart Internet as the company behind the app, one of several Flipkart companies in India. Since then it has absorbed its Singapore parents and become the owner of the rest. Its new accounts are the first to show the Flipkart group in India as one set of numbers, so the comparison with last year is the first thing to get right.

## Why Did Reported Revenue Jump Seven-Fold?

The short answer is that the company being measured changed. [Last year's teardown](/flipkart-fy25) covered Flipkart Internet and a small set of its own subsidiaries. In December 2025 a court-sanctioned scheme merged the Singapore holding companies into it, as described in [how the app became the parent](/flipkart-becomes-parent). Their Indian subsidiaries became its subsidiaries.

Three things in the FY2026 accounts confirm this is a real consolidation and not a scale error:

- **The parent now owns the group.** Flipkart Internet's own balance sheet carries ₹92,069 Cr of investments in subsidiaries, inside total assets of ₹110,896 Cr.
- **A goods business appeared.** Group revenue includes ₹104,850 Cr from sale of goods, and the group holds ₹13,609 Cr of inventory. A year ago the entity sold no goods and held no stock. The new line is most consistent with the wholesale business that used to sit in a sister company, though the data does not name subsidiaries.
- **Last year was restated.** The standalone cash flow opens FY2026 with ₹3,225 Cr of cash; Flipkart Internet reported ₹183 Cr at the end of FY2025. The group opens with ₹4,056 Cr against ₹187 Cr reported. The prior year has been redrawn as if the combination had always existed, the typical treatment for a merger within one ownership group.

Every total ties. Revenue lines add to ₹145,880 Cr, revenue plus ₹820 Cr of other income gives total income of ₹146,700 Cr, and share capital plus other equity matches reported equity. The numbers are in rupees and internally consistent. They simply describe a different company.

That has one awkward consequence. The restated FY2025 comparatives are not in the data we hold, so this piece does not compute a group growth rate. Any FY2025 figure quoted below is last year's reported number for the narrower entity.

## Where the Group Loses Money

The group spent ₹106.1 for every ₹100 of revenue. Two halves of the business behave very differently.

**Goods.** Purchases of traded goods were ₹117,617 Cr; after a ₹2,499 Cr build-up in inventory, the goods sold cost ₹115,118 Cr. Product sales were ₹104,850 Cr. On the face of the accounts, every ₹100 of goods revenue carried about ₹110 of goods cost, a ₹10,268 Cr gap before a single salary or delivery is paid.

The data does not split that line, so the reason needs care. Plausible contributors:

- Wholesale sales into the marketplace at thin or negative margins, with the group earning its return through fees instead.
- Discounting funded at the wholesale layer.
- Inventory write-downs or returns booked inside the goods cost line.

The first is the most consistent with how the two Flipkart companies worked before the merger, but the accounts do not prove it.

**Fees and services.** Services and other operating revenue came to ₹41,030 Cr. That line has to cover the goods gap and everything else: ₹15,154 Cr of transport and logistics, ₹8,376 Cr of staff cost, ₹4,941 Cr of advertising, ₹1,595 Cr of legal and professional charges and ₹1,559 Cr of technology spend.

This is the mechanism the old structure kept out of view. When the wholesale company and the app company reported separately, one showed the goods loss and the other showed the fees. Consolidated, the two sit on one page.

The bridge: depreciation and amortisation of ₹1,801 Cr (warehouses, equipment and leased sites; lease payments alone were ₹1,043 Cr), finance costs of ₹1,209 Cr, other income of ₹820 Cr, tax of ₹153 Cr and a ₹234 Cr share of losses in associates. Both readings are accurate. The second strips out the costs of the asset base and the funding structure; the first is what the owners bore. Either way, the group is loss-making at the operating level, and the net loss is 5.8% of revenue.

## The App Company Shrank, and Its Loss Grew

Flipkart Internet's standalone accounts are the closest thing to last year's subject. Revenue was ₹16,295 Cr, 20% below the ₹20,434 Cr reported for FY2025. The net loss was ₹5,498 Cr, 3.5x the ₹1,569 Cr reported. Both comparisons are against original, not restated, figures.

Two lines account for much of the loss and neither is cash:

- **Share-based payments of ₹1,958 Cr**, added back in the cash flow and almost half of the ₹4,287 Cr employee cost. Stock awards across the group are plausibly being charged at the new parent.
- **Net provisions of ₹2,351 Cr**, inside other expenses. The group's provisions were only ₹476 Cr, so most of the standalone charge disappears on consolidation. That pattern is consistent with provisions against amounts owed by, or invested in, group companies, but the accounts do not say so.

The second figure is not a like-for-like improvement claim: the FY2025 equivalents of both items are not in our data. It shows only that most of the jump in the standalone loss sits in two non-cash lines.

Why revenue fell is less clear. The accounts no longer split it by stream. Candidates include activities moving to other group companies, which fits last year's shift of logistics revenue to Ekart, changes in seller fees, and the restated base. Transport costs of ₹6,081 Cr still absorb 37% of standalone revenue, and legal and professional charges of ₹1,098 Cr are consistent with a year of cross-border merger work.

One more line deserves a flag. The standalone comprehensive result was a ₹1,154 Cr gain, because ₹7,206 Cr of currency translation gains came through other comprehensive income. That is most consistent with the merged Singapore company's dollar books being translated into rupees. It is not operating income.

## Borrowing Replaced Equity in FY2026

Last year, a ₹3,249 Cr rights issue from the Singapore parents funded the loss. In FY2026, neither set of cash flows shows any money from issuing shares.

The group used ₹6,525 Cr in operations. Inventory rose by ₹2,497 Cr, receivables by ₹1,799 Cr and other current assets by ₹2,871 Cr, partly offset by ₹3,358 Cr more owed to suppliers. The negative working-capital cushion of a pure marketplace no longer applies: this is now partly an inventory business. To fund it, the group drew ₹7,192 Cr of borrowings and ended the year owing ₹13,708 Cr.

The parent now acts as the group's treasury, the role Flipkart India played last year when it lent the app company ₹24,753 Cr. Flipkart Internet raised ₹14,976 Cr and repaid ₹7,170 Cr. It lent ₹29,983 Cr to other parties and got ₹29,348 Cr back, and put ₹6,178 Cr into subsidiaries. Its ₹6,826 Cr of long-term borrowings is identical in both sets of accounts, so that debt sits at the parent. The data does not identify the lenders.

## What Remains Unresolved

- **The restated FY2025 group.** Until it is in hand, nobody can say from these accounts how fast the combined business grew.
- **The goods gap.** Whether ₹110 of goods cost per ₹100 of goods revenue is by design or is narrowing decides whether fees can ever cover it.
- **Debt or equity next.** FY2026 ran on borrowing. Whether the parent recapitalises the group with equity is outside these accounts.
- **The standalone charges.** If share-based pay and provisions recur at this scale, the app company's loss stays well above last year's.

Flipkart now reports as one company. That model works only if fees grow faster than the losses on the goods sold underneath them.
