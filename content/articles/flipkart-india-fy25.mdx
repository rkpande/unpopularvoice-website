The Flipkart app does ₹20,493 Cr of annual revenue.

The Flipkart entity nobody talks about does ₹82,787 Cr.

That is Flipkart India Private Limited, the B2B wholesale and supplies arm of the Flipkart group. In FY25, it was four times larger by revenue than Flipkart Internet, the entity you actually see on your invoice. It also lost over three times as much money.

The size mismatch matters. When media coverage, analyst reports, or the forthcoming IPO prospectus reference "Flipkart's revenue," the number cited is almost always Flipkart Internet's, because that is the entity most readers associate with the brand. But the bigger entity, by revenue and by cumulative capital absorbed, is the one that sits underneath, moving physical inventory. Reading the group without reading this entity gives a systematically incomplete picture of how Flipkart actually makes money, where its working capital is tied up, and why the cumulative loss across the group is as large as it is.

Three lines in the audit tell you what happened.

## Line 1: Revenue from operations, ₹82,787 Cr

Up 17.3% from ₹70,542 Cr in FY24. The headline number, matching the audit's reported top line.

The number is big because Flipkart India buys physical inventory in bulk and sells it to marketplace sellers, who then list it onto the Flipkart app for consumer purchase. The entity records revenue when the inventory moves out, not when the consumer completes the transaction. In an inventory-led wholesale model, revenue tracks the volume of goods moving through the warehouse network, not the margin on each transaction.

By comparison, Flipkart Internet, the consumer-facing marketplace, records only commission and fees. The revenue line looks smaller because the economic structure is different. The marketplace entity is a platform. Flipkart India is an inventory wholesaler.

This split between a wholesale entity and a marketplace entity is specific to Indian e-commerce regulation. Under India's FDI policy, foreign-owned marketplace entities are not allowed to hold inventory or control pricing on items sold on their platform. They can only provide the platform itself. To work around this constraint without violating it, Flipkart (and Amazon, and most other FDI-funded e-commerce operators in India) uses a two-entity structure: a wholesale entity that holds inventory and sells it to arms-length sellers at wholesale prices, and a marketplace entity that lists those sellers' products and takes a commission. The sellers that buy from Flipkart India are frequently small merchants, including some that are heavily reliant on Flipkart India as their primary inventory source. That dependency is legal provided the sellers remain technically arms-length; it is policed by the Enforcement Directorate and the Commerce Ministry, both of which have periodically investigated FDI-compliance across e-commerce operators in recent years.

The economic effect is that the gross merchandise value that flows through the Flipkart platform passes through two legal entities. Flipkart India records the first revenue (wholesale sale to the seller). The marketplace entity records the second (commission on the seller's retail sale). The combined revenue is not the sum of the two entities; it is one economic transaction split across two books, and consolidation eliminates most of the overlap.

## Line 2: Net loss, ₹5,189 Cr

Up 22% from ₹4,248 Cr in FY24. The widest annual loss on the entity's recent record.

This is a different direction of travel from the sister entity. Flipkart Internet narrowed its FY25 loss 37%. Flipkart India widened its FY25 loss 22%. On revenue growth of 14% and 17% respectively. Same group. Same brand. Opposite direction on the P&L line that most readers watch.

The widening is not a surprise given the business model. In an inventory-led wholesale operation, loss expansion with revenue is the structural norm. The entity buys at wholesale prices, carries inventory risk, pays warehousing and logistics cost, and sells at a thin spread. When the top line grows 17%, inventory cost grows at roughly the same rate. There is no operating leverage to speak of at this layer of the business.

The multi-year trajectory confirms this structural reading. In FY22, Flipkart India posted a loss of ₹3,404 Cr. In FY23, the loss widened to ₹4,891 Cr. In FY24, it narrowed slightly to ₹4,248 Cr. In FY25, it widened to ₹5,189 Cr. The three-year range is ₹3,400 Cr to ₹5,200 Cr. There is no loss-compression trajectory visible at this entity. There is a loss band that moves with inventory volume. The FY25 figure is at the upper end of the band, driven by inventory purchases that scaled 18% year-on-year.

## Line 3: Stock-in-trade purchases, ₹87,738 Cr

Up 18.1% from ₹74,271 Cr in FY24. The single largest line in the entire P&L, exceeding the total revenue of operations.

That is the mechanical reason loss widens with revenue. Flipkart India spent ₹87,738 Cr buying inventory in FY25. It booked ₹82,787 Cr of revenue selling that inventory onward. The gap, before any other operating cost, is already structurally negative.

Add employee cost, warehousing, logistics, and finance cost on top, and the FY25 total expense base reaches ₹88,121 Cr. Against ₹83,105 Cr of total revenue (including other income), the arithmetic produces the ₹5,189 Cr loss.

The gross spread between stock-in-trade purchases and wholesale revenue (₹87,738 Cr vs ₹82,787 Cr) represents approximately ₹4,951 Cr of negative gross contribution before any other operating cost. In normal wholesale distribution businesses, gross contribution is positive; merchants mark up their wholesale sale prices above their purchase prices. In Flipkart India's case, the negative gross contribution reflects two factors. The first is inventory price erosion: goods bought at the start of the year sell at lower-than-purchase prices by year-end, particularly in electronics, which moves fast on price. The second is slow-moving inventory write-downs: unsold stock is marked down at year-end to reflect its realizable value. The audit discloses specific inventory provisioning charges that contribute to the gap.

In a steady-state wholesale business, this gap would be a crisis signal. In Flipkart India's case, it is a structural feature of the business model: Flipkart India is not primarily a profit-seeking wholesaler. It is an inventory-management utility for the marketplace. Its role is to carry inventory risk that the marketplace entity cannot (under FDI rules), and it is funded by Walmart to do so.

## Why finance costs grew 57%

Finance costs at Flipkart India rose from around ₹289 Cr to ₹454 Cr in FY25, a 57% jump. On a loss trajectory that is widening, the next instinct is to check whether this represents a new external bank facility. It does not.

The majority of the finance cost line represents intercompany interest paid upstream to Walmart's Singapore-incorporated holding entities, which fund the inventory cycle through intercompany advances. As the inventory cycle grew, intercompany borrowing grew with it, and so did the interest paid.

That intercompany interest flows out of Flipkart India and into Flipkart Marketplace Private Limited and Flipkart Private Limited, both Singapore-incorporated. From there, it rolls up to Walmart Inc. The audit records the India-side finance cost. The Walmart consolidated financials net out the full flow.

The specific interest rate charged on the intercompany loans sits in the audit's related-party-transactions note. For FY25 the applied rate was in the range of 7-9% on the outstanding intercompany debt, broadly in line with commercial borrowing rates in India at the time. That rate matters for transfer-pricing scrutiny: the Income Tax department reviews whether intercompany interest rates reflect arm's-length pricing (what a third-party lender would charge). If the rate is above arm's-length, the Indian entity's reported finance cost is artificially high, which pulls Indian taxable income down and shifts profit offshore. If it is below arm's-length, the opposite effect applies. Flipkart India's rate is defensible on arm's-length criteria, which is why it has not attracted significant transfer-pricing disputes in the public record, but it is a line that will continue to be reviewed as the entity's intercompany debt grows.

## The working-capital cycle, in detail

Inventory-led businesses live or die by working-capital management. The specific cycle at Flipkart India:

The entity buys inventory from manufacturers and wholesalers on credit terms that typically range from 30 to 60 days. Trade payables at year-end stood at a material fraction of the annual stock-in-trade purchase figure, reflecting that significant inventory is being held on supplier credit at any given point.

The inventory then moves to Flipkart India's warehouses. The average inventory holding period, inferred from the ratio of closing inventory to daily cost of goods sold, is in the range of 30-45 days.

The inventory is then sold to marketplace sellers (who are often small traders using Flipkart India as a drop-ship source). The receivable from the seller is typically paid within 15-30 days.

The net working-capital cycle, from cash out to cash in, is roughly 45-75 days. On an annual inventory throughput of ₹87,738 Cr, every 15 days of working-capital extension represents approximately ₹3,600 Cr of additional cash tied up. This is why Flipkart India needs continuous intercompany funding: not because the entity is leaking cash operationally, but because inventory growth continuously absorbs working capital.

Compare this to a pure marketplace entity like Flipkart Internet, which has effectively zero inventory, minimal physical working capital, and a revenue-to-receivables ratio that releases cash as the business grows. The two entities have opposite working-capital profiles. One absorbs cash with growth. The other releases it. Both are inside the same group, serving the same consumers, selling the same products.

## Why reading Flipkart only through the marketplace misses the half that matters

Most press coverage of Flipkart centers on Flipkart Internet, because that is the consumer-facing entity, the app, and the one that most readers interact with. The FY25 numbers show why that framing is incomplete.

Flipkart India generated ₹62,294 Cr more revenue than Flipkart Internet in FY25 alone. It lost ₹3,695 Cr more. It booked ₹5,951 Cr more in finance cost and inventory handling than Flipkart Internet's entire finance cost line.

Yet most of the strategic narrative, the IPO prep, the loss-narrowing story, is attached to Flipkart Internet. That is understandable: Flipkart Internet is now the principal holding company post-flip, and the one that will be listed. But treating it as the whole of Flipkart's economic footprint in India misses the larger engine that funds the inventory cycle underneath it.

## What the Walmart funding model looks like on the register

For a group of Flipkart India's revenue scale, posting a ₹5,189 Cr annual loss, the next question is the balance sheet. Normally, an Indian wholesale and distribution operation of this scale would carry a mix of external working-capital lines (bank facilities, NBFC inventory-financing, trade credit) secured against receivables and stock.

For Flipkart India, that picture is largely absent on the external register. The ₹454 Cr of finance cost is predominantly intercompany. The funding structure is parent-driven, flowing from Walmart Inc. through Singapore holding entities into the India entity as a mix of equity and intercompany debt. External secured facilities are not materially present.

That is the parent-funded shape. The trade-off is not on the P&L. The trade-off is that the business is strategically dependent on continued Walmart willingness to fund the inventory cycle at this scale, through the Singapore holding chain, year on year. The FY25 numbers show that willingness remained intact. The post-flip FY26 audit, when it lands, will record whether the architecture around that willingness has moved as well.

Post-flip, the funding architecture simplifies but does not go away. The Singapore holding entities are gone. Walmart's capital injections will now flow directly from Walmart Inc. (U.S.A.) through the appropriate FDI-compliant channels into Flipkart Internet (which is now the apex Indian entity), which will then on-lend or on-invest into Flipkart India as a subsidiary. The economic substance is identical; the legal pathway is one layer shorter.

## What happens to this entity after the IPO

A legitimate question: does Flipkart India itself go public? The answer appears to be no, at least not directly. The IPO path currently discussed in press coverage involves Flipkart Internet, the apex entity, listing on an Indian exchange. Flipkart India would continue as a wholly-owned subsidiary, with its P&L consolidating into the group financials but not trading its own shares.

For public-market investors, this means the Flipkart India P&L shape (inventory-led, loss-widening with growth) becomes a line item inside the group consolidation rather than a separately traded entity. Investors will see it as segment data (if Flipkart discloses wholesale as a reportable segment) or as an elimination item in the related-party-transactions note. The ₹5,189 Cr standalone loss will be visible in the group filings but will not be the number that drives the share price on day one of listing.

That said, the investor community following Indian e-commerce IPOs has historically scrutinized the two-entity structure carefully. Any change to FDI regulation (which has been under periodic review by DPIIT for several years) could reshape what Flipkart India is allowed to do, with consequent impact on the group's operational and financial structure. This is the most significant regulatory risk that attaches specifically to the Flipkart India entity.

## The read the audit gives you

Flipkart India filed. The numbers are the numbers. Revenue grew 17%. Loss widened 22%. Stock-in-trade purchases rose 18%. Finance cost rose 57%. The entity is the wholesale and supplies backbone of the Flipkart group, and its P&L shape reflects that function, not the function of the consumer marketplace that sits on top of it.

Reading Flipkart without reading Flipkart India misses the bigger half of the group by revenue, and misses the structural reason the marketplace entity above can post the loss-narrowing numbers it does. The wholesale layer is doing some of the heavy lifting that lets the marketplace layer look better.

Both audits are on the public record. Both numbers are the numbers. The structure that connects them is the piece most of the coverage does not surface.
