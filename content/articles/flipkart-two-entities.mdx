You open the Flipkart app.

There is no single company called Flipkart.

The brand is a customer-facing surface. The business runs on two separate private limited companies, both incorporated in Karnataka, both ultimately held by Walmart Inc., and each filing its own audited P&L every year.

In FY25, one of them cut its loss 37%. The other one widened its loss 22%.

## In FY25, the two entities moved in opposite directions

For most of the Flipkart story so far, the two entities have moved broadly together: both scaling, both loss-making, both absorbing Walmart equity. FY25 was the first year on recent record where the two P&L directions diverged.

Flipkart Internet revenue grew 14% and loss narrowed 37%. Flipkart India revenue grew 17% and loss widened 22%. Both top lines moved in the same direction. The bottom lines did not.

## Why reading only one entity misses the business

Adding the two standalone P&Ls would give around ₹1,03,280 Cr of reported revenue and roughly ₹6,683 Cr of combined loss. That addition would be wrong.

The reason is intercompany flow. Flipkart India's ₹82,787 Cr of revenue includes the inventory it sells into the Flipkart marketplace. When Flipkart Internet books commission on those sales, part of the economic value is already recorded upstream at Flipkart India. Only Walmart's consolidated view, at the group level, eliminates that double-count.

What the two standalone audits let you do is see the shape of each operating engine separately: how much is the consumer marketplace growing (or shrinking), and how much is the wholesale and supplies engine.

In FY25, those two engines look different.

## The marketplace: a real operational improvement

Flipkart Internet's loss narrowed 37% in FY25, from ₹2,359 Cr to ₹1,494 Cr. That did not happen by accident. It happened on three visible levers inside the audit.

The first is revenue mix. Marketplace services (commission on seller sales) more than doubled to ₹7,751 Cr, up from ₹3,734 Cr. Advertising revenue grew 27% to ₹6,317 Cr. These are high-margin platform fees. Together they contributed ₹5,361 Cr of incremental revenue in a single year.

The second is logistics income. It fell 38%, from ₹6,838 Cr to ₹4,225 Cr. This is the structural shift: shipping fees that used to sit in the marketplace entity now flow through Ekart, the Flipkart logistics arm, which files its own P&L. The marketplace entity still books the economics but no longer the top line.

The third is employee cost. Employee benefit expense was actually cut, from ₹5,178 Cr to ₹4,748 Cr, an 8% reduction while revenue grew 14%. That is the kind of cost-base movement that compresses losses faster than revenue can.

## The wholesale: a widening loss on the inventory layer

Flipkart India sits on the other side of the picture. Its FY25 revenue grew 17% to ₹82,787 Cr. Its loss widened 22% to ₹5,189 Cr. The audit's total expenses line rose 17.4% to ₹88,121 Cr. The single biggest cost line, stock-in-trade purchases, rose from ₹74,271 Cr to ₹87,738 Cr. Finance costs rose 57% to ₹454 Cr.

That is the shape of a wholesale and supplies business. Flipkart India buys physical inventory at scale, holds it, and sells it onwards to the marketplace sellers. The gross spread on inventory-led commerce is thin by definition. The entity's reported loss is a function of inventory risk, warehousing cost, and the structurally rising finance cost that funds the inventory cycle.

Reading Flipkart only through Flipkart Internet misses this engine entirely. Reading it only through Flipkart India misses the consumer-facing marketplace.

## The intercompany flow that connects them

The two entities are not independent. In FY25, Flipkart Internet took ₹24,753 Cr of demand loans from Flipkart India (its fellow subsidiary) and repaid ₹24,133 Cr during the same year. The net outstanding at year-end was modest. The gross flow is what matters: nearly ₹24,753 Cr of intercompany funding moved in a single year between the two entities.

That flow is one of the reasons their P&Ls look so different. The wholesale entity carries the funding burden of the inventory cycle. The marketplace entity receives working capital against that flow. The finance costs sit on the wholesale side (₹454 Cr, up 57%). The operational improvement visibly lands on the marketplace side.

Flipkart Internet also raised ₹3,249 Cr via a rights issue during FY25 from its Singapore parent entities. The audit note is explicit: this is Walmart equity, flowing through Flipkart Marketplace Private Limited and Flipkart Private Limited (both Singapore) into the India operating entity.

## The post-balance-sheet event that changed the group shape

The FY25 audits capture the state of play up to 31 March 2025. The group architecture has changed materially since then.

In April 2025, the Flipkart Internet board approved a composite Scheme of Arrangement. The scheme was lodged with the NCLT Bengaluru bench and the General Division of the High Court of the Republic of Singapore on 22 May 2025. The scheme covered eight group entities: Flipkart Health, Quickroutes International, Flipkart Marketplace (Singapore), FK Myntra Holdings, Flipkart Investments, KLICK2SHOP Logistics Services International, Flippay, and Flipkart Private Limited (Singapore).

The NCLT principal bench sanctioned the scheme in December 2025. The flip completed in March 2026. Flipkart Internet Private Limited is now the principal holding company for the entire Flipkart group in India, ahead of a planned Indian IPO.

Also recorded in the audit as subsequent events: an allotment of 24.7 million additional Class A equity shares via a further rights issue, and a bonus issue of 2.91 billion Class A shares and 12.4 million Class B shares in a 101:1 ratio by capitalisation of the securities premium account.

## The debt profile is what the Walmart funding model looks like

For a group of Flipkart's scale, posting the combined losses it posted in FY25, the next line to check is the external debt register. For most Indian consumer-tech operators carrying this loss trajectory, the register typically shows a rotating set of working-capital lines with scheduled banks, NBFC facilities renewed each year, and term loans secured against receivables or inventory.

For both Flipkart entities, that picture is largely absent.

The ₹454 Cr of finance cost at Flipkart India in FY25 is predominantly intercompany interest paid upstream to Walmart-controlled Singapore entities. The ₹162 Cr of finance cost at Flipkart Internet includes a significant intercompany-interest component, with the balance being lease liability charges and limited working-capital charges. The equity base has absorbed the losses. External secured debt has not been expanded materially through the FY23-to-FY25 loss cycle.

That is the Walmart funding model on the register. The parent provides capital as equity and intercompany debt rather than exposing the operating entities to third-party servicing obligations.

## The read the two audits give you

Reading Flipkart by looking only at Flipkart Internet misses the wholesale engine entirely. Reading it by looking only at Flipkart India misses the consumer-facing marketplace. Adding the two double-counts the goods that move between them.

The genuine forensic read of Flipkart in FY25 is that both operating entities file, both stayed current, and both moved in opposite directions on the loss line. Revenue grew at both. Loss narrowed at the marketplace. Loss widened at the wholesaler. ₹24,753 Cr of intercompany demand loans flowed between them in a single year. Walmart equity absorbed the shortfall at both. External secured debt was not granted or drawn against either.

The next audit will be filed under a different group shape. The Singapore holding entities are gone. Flipkart Internet, in India, is the parent now. The FY25 audits are the last snapshot of the pre-flip architecture.

Everything above is on the public record. The audits landed. The numbers are the numbers. The structure is the story most readers do not see.
